Case Study

Desert Shore Community Baptist Church: MEP, Fire Alarm and Structural Engineering

Bullhead City, Arizona | Church | 25,416 sq ft | Plan review approved

Desert Shore Community Baptist Church in Bullhead City, Arizona is cleared to build. TS9Designs handled the mechanical, electrical, plumbing, fire alarm/life safety and structural engineering for the 25,416 sq ft building, and the plans were approved in plan review.

A church is an assembly occupancy. That raises the bar on egress, fire alarm, ventilation and structural design compared with an office or retail space of the same size. The electrical service, gas piping, site lighting and the fire alarm interface with the HVAC all had to line up before plan review would sign off. They did.

The Challenge

A 25,416 sq ft church is not one room. It is a sanctuary that fills on a schedule, plus the classrooms, offices, kitchen, restrooms and gathering spaces around it, and a parking lot that empties all at once after a service. Each of those drives a different engineering requirement, and in plan review the reviewer checks that they agree with each other.

The disciplines also depend on each other. The electrical service has to carry the HVAC, lighting, kitchen and site loads. The gas piping has to be sized for the equipment the mechanical plans call for. The fire alarm has to control the HVAC. The structure has to carry the rooftop equipment and the long spans of the sanctuary. If one discipline changes late, the others move with it. For this project TS9Designs held all five under one roof, so that coordination happened in-house rather than between separate firms.

Design Strategy

Electrical: a 1,200-amp service

The building was designed around a 1,200-amp electrical service. For a church, the service has to cover peak Sunday conditions: the sanctuary HVAC running at full load, house and stage lighting on, sound and AV equipment, a kitchen in use, and the full parking lot lit after an evening service. The service size is set by a demand calculation that adds those loads with the diversity the code allows, and plan review checks that calculation against the panel schedules and the single-line diagram.

Getting the service right at the design stage matters because it fixes the utility transformer, the main switchboard and the feeder sizes. Undersize it and the owner pays for a service upgrade later. Oversize it and the switchgear and utility fees go up for capacity that is never used.

Site lighting: every parking space and walkway

Site lighting was laid out so that every parking space and walkway is lit correctly. For an assembly building this is a life-safety item, not just a convenience. Hundreds of people leave at the same time, often after dark, and the path from the exit doors to the cars is part of the egress route. A photometric layout sets pole locations, fixture types and mounting heights so the lot meets minimum light levels and uniformity without spilling light onto neighbouring property.

Mechanical: HVAC for a space that fills and empties

Sanctuary HVAC is sized for a high occupant density that only exists for a few hours a week, which pushes up the outdoor air and cooling requirements compared with the rest of the building. In the Arizona climate the cooling load dominates. The mechanical design sets the equipment, the airflow to each space, and the ventilation rates for assembly use, and it coordinates with the structural plans for the equipment supports and with the electrical plans for the loads.

Plumbing and gas

Gas piping was sized and calculated for the building. A gas sizing calculation lists every appliance, its input in BTU/h and its distance from the meter, then sizes each pipe segment so the furthest appliance still receives gas at the right pressure under full demand. Plan review expects that calculation on the drawings. Plumbing for an assembly occupancy also means fixture counts set by the occupant load of the sanctuary, which is typically far higher than the floor area alone would suggest.

Fire alarm and life safety

The fire alarm was tied into the HVAC. In an alarm condition the fire alarm panel signals the air handling units to shut down, so that the duct system does not move smoke through the building while people are leaving. On larger units a duct smoke detector triggers the shutdown directly. This interface is one of the items plan review looks for specifically on an assembly building, because it has to appear consistently on the fire alarm plans, the mechanical plans and the electrical plans.

Structural

Structural engineering covered the building itself. A church typically has a sanctuary with long clear spans and a higher roof than the rest of the building, which drives the framing design, and rooftop mechanical equipment that the structure has to carry. The structural set and the mechanical set were coordinated so that equipment locations and weights on the roof plan match the structural framing plan.

Coordination and Plan Review

Each of these items is straightforward on its own. The work is in making them agree. The service size has to match the connected load on the mechanical and lighting schedules. The gas calculation has to match the equipment schedule. The fire alarm shutdown has to be shown on three different sets of drawings and say the same thing on each. Reviewers check for exactly these cross-references, and a mismatch is a comment that costs a review cycle.

Because TS9Designs carried all five disciplines on this project, those cross-checks happened before the set was submitted rather than being flagged by the reviewer. The plans were prepared for permitting and include stamps. The plans were approved in plan review and the church is cleared to build.

Outcomes

The plans were approved in plan review, and Desert Shore Community Baptist Church is cleared to build. The owner has a coordinated, stamped set in which the electrical, mechanical, plumbing, fire alarm and structural work has already been reconciled, which reduces the field questions and change orders that come from drawings prepared by separate firms.

What to know before starting a church project

  • Treat the sanctuary as an assembly occupancy from the first meeting. Its occupant load drives egress, fixture counts, ventilation and fire alarm scope.
  • Settle the electrical service early. It fixes the utility transformer and switchgear, and it is the hardest item to change after permit.
  • Plan site lighting with the egress routes, not just the parking count. The path from the doors to the cars is part of the life-safety design.
  • Expect plan review to ask for the gas sizing calculation and the fire alarm to HVAC shutdown on the drawings. Have both ready at first submittal.
  • Keep the rooftop equipment list in front of the structural engineer. Equipment weights and locations change the roof framing.
  • Using one firm for MEP, fire alarm and structural removes the hand-offs where most coordination errors start.
